1 · The Odyssey (Week 1)

Revision notes. The whole poem, by the arguments examiners set. For each one, learn two scenes from the first half and two from the second, with the book number.

The shape

Books 1–4, the Telemachy (Book 1 is prescribed): Ithaca without its king. Books 5–8: Calypso, the raft, Nausicaa, the Phaeacians. Books 9–12, the apologoi: Odysseus tells his own wanderings. Book 13: home, and Athena. Books 16–23: disguise, testing, recognition, revenge, reunion. Prescribed: 1, 5–13, 16–19, 21–23.

The second half, book by book

16. In Eumaeus’ hut Athena restores Odysseus’ looks and he reveals himself to Telemachus, who at first thinks he is a god. They weep like birds robbed of their young. The suitors’ ambush has failed; Penelope rebukes Antinous.

17. The beggar walks to town. The goatherd Melanthius kicks him. The old dog Argos, lying on a dung-heap, knows him, wags his tail and dies. In the hall Antinous throws a footstool.

18. The beggar boxes Irus. Penelope, made beautiful by Athena, shows herself to the suitors and draws gifts from them; Odysseus is pleased. The maid Melantho insults him. Eurymachus throws a stool.

19. The interview by the fire. Odysseus tells Penelope a Cretan tale; she weeps like melting snow. Eurycleia washes his feet and knows the scar (the boar on Parnassus, his grandfather Autolycus); he takes her by the throat. Penelope’s dream of the eagle and the geese; the gates of horn and ivory; she announces the contest of the bow.

21. The bow and the twelve axes. Telemachus nearly strings it. Odysseus reveals himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius by the scar. He strings the bow as a bard strings a lyre, and shoots through the axes.

22. Antinous is shot through the throat as he lifts his cup. Eurymachus offers compensation and is refused. Melanthius arms the suitors; Athena appears as Mentor and then raises the aegis. The priest Leodes is killed; the bard Phemius and the herald Medon are spared. Twelve maids are hanged by Telemachus; Melanthius is mutilated. Eurycleia is forbidden to gloat.

23. Penelope will not believe it. She tests him by telling Eurycleia to move the bed; he is angry, and describes how he built it round a living olive tree. The simile of shipwrecked sailors reaching land is given to her. Athena holds back the dawn.

The arguments

Odysseus as hero. Mêtis (the Cyclops, the Cretan lies, the bow) against bia; endurance; but pride (the boast to Polyphemus), secrecy with his crew (Scylla), and a revenge that includes the maids.

Xenia. Good hosts: Nestor, Menelaus, the Phaeacians, Eumaeus. Bad hosts: Polyphemus. Bad guests: the suitors. Zeus is the god of guests, which is how the poem justifies the slaughter.

Women. Penelope matches him in cunning (the web, the gifts, the bed). Athena directs the plot. Calypso and Circe delay and help. Eurycleia and Eumaeus show loyalty in slaves; Melantho and the twelve show what disloyalty costs.

Recognition. Telemachus (16), Argos (17), Eurycleia (19), the herdsmen (21), the suitors (22), Penelope (23). Each uses a different token: appearance, instinct, the scar, the bow, the bed.

Justice. Zeus in Book 1: mortals suffer through their own recklessness. The suitors were warned repeatedly (Halitherses, Theoclymenus, the beggar himself). Against that: Amphinomus and Leodes die with the worst, and the maids were slaves.

2 · Sappho (Week 1)

Revision notes. Use your OCR booklet of the prescribed poems alongside this page. Learn each poem by number, by one image and by what it shows about desire.

Context

Who and when. Sappho of Lesbos (Mytilene), around 600 BC. Lyric poetry: sung to the lyre, in the Aeolic dialect, some for solo performance and some (the wedding songs) for a chorus. Nine books were collected at Alexandria; almost all is lost.

Fragments. Only poem 1 is complete. Poem 31 breaks off; others are a line or two, quoted by later writers or found on papyrus. Every claim about Sappho has to allow for what is missing: we often do not know the speaker, the occasion or the ending.

Audience. Debated: a circle of young women before marriage, a chorus she trained, or a symposium. The poems speak of women by name (Anactoria, Atthis, Gongyla) and of marriage as loss.

The poems to know

1, the hymn to Aphrodite. A prayer in the proper form: the goddess addressed with her titles, reminded of past help, asked for help now. Aphrodite came before in a chariot drawn by sparrows, smiled, and asked who wrongs you, Sappho? She promised that the one who flees will soon pursue. Sappho asks her to be her ally in battle. Humour and self-knowledge: the goddess has heard this before.

2. An invitation to Aphrodite to come from Crete to a sacred grove: apple trees, altars smoking with incense, cold water, roses, sleep falling from the leaves. Desire as a place and as ritual.

16. A priamel: some say horsemen, some infantry, some ships are the finest thing on the black earth; I say it is whatever one loves. Proof: Helen, who left the best of husbands. So the speaker thinks of Anactoria, who is absent, and would rather see her walk than the chariots of Lydia. War’s values answered with love’s.

31. A man sits opposite a woman and seems equal to the gods. The speaker watches, and her body fails: no voice, tongue broken, fire under the skin, eyes blind, ears humming, sweat, trembling, greener than grass, close to death. Desire as a list of symptoms. The last surviving words turn towards endurance. Imitated by Catullus (51).

94. A parting. A woman leaves weeping; the speaker tells her to go and remember: garlands of violets and roses, perfume, soft beds. Memory as consolation. The first surviving line, I wish I were dead, may be either woman’s.

130. Eros the limb-loosener shakes me again, a sweet-bitter creature that cannot be fought (glukupikron). Anne Carson built her account of desire as lack on this one word.

The wedding songs. Praise of bride and groom, jokes at the doorkeeper’s expense, the bride compared to an apple out of reach. A public, communal voice beside the private one.

The arguments

Desire is physical and involuntary (31, 130) and also chosen and valued (16). Love is between women, and is not presented as shameful. The gods are close: Aphrodite talks to her. Marriage means separation (94). Against Plato: Sappho stays with one beloved body and its loss; Diotima tells the lover to climb away from it. Against Ovid: Sappho speaks from inside desire; Ovid teaches how to manage it.

3 · Plato, Symposium (Week 1)

Revision notes. Know who says what. Marks are lost by giving Aristophanes’ myth to Socrates, or Pausanias’ two Aphrodites to Diotima.

Context

Plato, Athenian, pupil of Socrates, writing in the fourth century BC. The Symposium is set at a drinking party at the house of the tragic poet Agathon, who has just won his first victory (416 BC). The guests agree to drink lightly and each give a speech in praise of Eros. The setting is the world of elite Athenian men, and the love mostly in view is pederasty: an older lover (erastês) and a younger beloved (erômenos).

The speeches in order

Phaedrus. Eros is the oldest of the gods and the cause of the greatest goods: a lover is ashamed to be seen acting badly by his beloved, so an army of lovers would be unbeatable. Examples: Alcestis died for her husband; Achilles died to avenge his lover Patroclus; Orpheus would not die and was shown a phantom.

Pausanias. There are two Aphrodites and so two loves. Common (Pandemos) love is of the body, of women as much as boys, and does not last. Heavenly (Urania) love is of the mind and character, between males, and aims at virtue. A relationship is honourable if the beloved yields for the sake of becoming better.

Eryximachus, the doctor. Love is a force in all nature: medicine, music and the seasons depend on the harmony of opposites.

Aristophanes, the comic poet (after his hiccups). Humans were once round, with four arms and four legs, in three kinds: male, female and androgynous. They attacked the gods; Zeus cut them in half. Each half searches for its other half; love is the name for the desire and pursuit of the whole. Hephaestus might offer to weld two lovers into one. The myth accounts for love between men, between women and between men and women.

Agathon. Eros is the youngest, most beautiful and most delicate of the gods, and has every virtue. Socrates questions him: if love desires beauty, love lacks it.

Socrates, reporting Diotima, a wise woman of Mantinea. Eros is neither god nor mortal but a daimon, a spirit between the two. He is the child of Poros (Resource) and Penia (Poverty), conceived at the feast for Aphrodite’s birth: always in need, always scheming. Love is desire for the permanent possession of the good, and its work is giving birth in beauty, in body (children) or in soul (virtue, laws, poems). Then the ladder: from one beautiful body, to all beautiful bodies, to beautiful souls, to laws and practices, to knowledge, to the Form of Beauty itself, which does not change.

Alcibiades bursts in drunk and praises Socrates instead: he is like a statue of Silenus that opens to show gods inside; he resisted Alcibiades’ attempt to seduce him under one cloak; he was brave at Potidaea and Delium. The ladder in practice, or its human cost.

The arguments

Love as lack. Aristophanes: we lack our other half. Diotima: Eros lacks the good and the beautiful. Compare Sappho 130 and 31.

Body and soul. Pausanias ranks them; Diotima makes the body only the first step. Is the individual beloved left behind? Many readers think so.

Whose view is it? Plato writes no speech in his own voice. Diotima’s teaching comes through Socrates, through a narrator, years later. K. J. Dover set the speeches in the conventions of Athenian homosexual relations, which Pausanias describes most directly.

Men and women. The speakers are all men; the highest teaching is given to a woman, Diotima; women’s desire appears only in Aristophanes’ myth.

4 · Ovid, Ars Amatoria 3 (Week 2)

Revision notes (to 3.644, as far as we have read). Use your Kline text and the Reading Notes alongside this page.

Context

Ovid (43 BC–AD 17), writing in Rome under Augustus. The Ars Amatoria appeared about 2 BC–AD 2: Books 1 and 2 teach men how to find and keep a woman; Book 3 teaches women. It is a didactic poem, the form of Hesiod and of Virgil’s Georgics, but in elegiac couplets and on a subject that makes the form a joke: Ovid is the teacher of love. Augustus’ marriage and adultery laws of 18 BC made adultery a crime. In AD 8 Ovid was banished to Tomis for a poem and a mistake; the poem was this one.

Book 3 in order

Opening. He has armed the Greeks against the Amazons; now he will arm the Amazons. Not all women are bad: Penelope, Laodamia, Alcestis. Men deceive more often than women: Jason, Theseus, Demophoon, and Aeneas, who gave Dido the sword and the reason to die (3.39–42). Venus told him to teach.

Carpe diem. Use your youth: the years go like running water, and the day comes when you will lie cold and alone.

Cultus. Once there was rough simplicity; now Rome is golden (3.113). He welcomes his own age because it has refinement. Hair should suit the shape of the face (3.133–168). Dress: no need for purple and gold; choose the colour that suits you (3.169–192).

The body. No rank armpits or rough legs; clean teeth. Make-up is recommended but the process must be hidden: let him think you are asleep. Much is ugly in the doing and pleasing when done (3.193–250).

Hiding faults. The short woman should sit; the thin should wear thick cloth; learn how to laugh, and how to cry, and how to walk (3.251–310).

Accomplishments. Sing: the Sirens held ships by their voices (3.311). Play the lyre. Read the poets: Callimachus, Anacreon, Sappho (what is more wanton than she? 3.331), Menander, Propertius, Gallus, Tibullus, Virgil, and Ovid himself. Dance; know the board games.

Be seen. The porticoes, the theatres, the temples, the Circus: what is hidden is unknown.

Beware. Avoid the man who is too well groomed, and thieves who are after your clothes (3.433–466). Letters: send them by a trusted maid, make him wait a little, disguise your hand (3.467–498).

Temper. Anger disfigures: Pallas threw away her pipes when she saw her puffed cheeks in the water (3.505). Do not be proud or gloomy.

Managing lovers. Give each man a role that fits him; value poets, who give fame. Treat the novice and the veteran differently. Let him believe he has a rival; shut the door sometimes. Why are wives not loved? Because their husbands may come whenever they like (3.585–586). Drop the blunted foils (3.589).

Deceiving the guard. Letters carried on a maid’s body or written in milk; and the city of alibis: theatres, the Circus, the temple of the Good Goddess, the baths (to 3.644).

The arguments

Is Book 3 on women’s side? He claims to arm them, defends them from the charge of treachery, and assumes they have desires and choices. But every instruction makes a woman more pleasing to men, and the teacher keeps reminding us he has taught the other side too. Roy Gibson, in his commentary, shows how often the advice steers women to a middle way: neither too plain nor too showy.

Love as art. For Sappho desire strikes; for Diotima it climbs; for Ovid it is a skill with rules, practised in the city. Is it serious? The mock-didactic voice, the myths cut down to jokes (Pallas, the Sirens), and the cheerful advice on deceiving husbands sit badly beside Augustus’ laws.

The arguments

Is it a story of growing naturalism? From pattern (New York Kouros) to observed anatomy (Anavysos, Aristodikos), to weight-shift (Doryphoros), to the body in the round (Apoxyomenos). But the Canon is an ideal and not a portrait; the fourth century adds mood and softness and not more accuracy; and the kouroi were never trying to be lifelike. E. H. Gombrich called the change the Greek revolution; J. J. Pollitt described the move from character (ethos) to emotion (pathos).

Material matters. Bronze allows open poses (the Zeus, the Charioteer’s arm); marble needs supports (the tree trunk of the Hermes). Most famous bronzes survive only as Roman marble copies, so say ‘copy’ when it is one.

Filling the space. A pediment is a triangle: Corcyra changes scale; Aigina uses fallen and crouching fighters; Olympia and the Parthenon use reclining figures at the corners. A metope is a square: two or three figures, strongest when they cross (the Cretan Bull, Parthenon XXVII). A frieze is a continuous band: procession or battle.

6 · Using the scholars (both weeks)

Revision notes. A scholar’s name earns nothing by being mentioned. It earns marks when you say what the scholar argues, test it against the text, and say whether you agree. This tab gives you one or two scholars for each topic, ten quick questions on what they argue, and five short written responses.

The four moves

1. Claim. Put the scholar’s argument in your own words, in one sentence. 2. For. Give one moment from the text, or one thing you can see in the statue, that supports it. 3. Against. Give one that does not fit. 4. Judgement. Say how far you agree, and why.

The scholars

Odyssey. Joel Christensen (2025): xenia is the poem’s moral measure, and Odysseus does not always pass it: “Odysseus himself demands a guest gift from Polyphemus after he has already plundered his household.” Patrice Rankine (2025): “The men tell stories. Women weave, but both activities require mētis, cunning intelligence.” Use Rankine for Penelope as Odysseus’ equal.

Sappho. Miroslav Marcovich (1972) on poem 31: the message is more likely ‘I cannot help being in love with you’ than jealousy of the man, who is there to show the woman’s effect. G. L. Koniaris (1967) on poem 16: Sappho defines the most beautiful thing as “whatever a person loves”, a claim about any lover, man or woman, and not a woman’s reply to men. Odysseus Tsagarakis (1979): do not assume the ‘I’ of a lyric poem is the poet reporting her own life; the poem “is art, not a recording of live experience”.

Plato. F. C. White (1989): the usual view is that the lover’s final object is beauty, but for Diotima beauty “is always subordinate to that of the good”. Gregory Vlastos, as reported by White and Levy: Platonic love treats the individual as a means, a step to be used and left. Martin Warner (1979) replies that if a person is his qualities, then to love the qualities is to love the person.

Ovid. Peter Jones (2005): the love poems treat their subject “lightly, wittily and ironically”, and “parody and self-mockery are well to the fore”; Ovid was “indifferent to, even defiant of, political projects” at a time of laws on morals. Philip Hardie (2002): Ovid “has often been accused of mocking and trivializing love”; Hardie wants to move beyond “the stereotype of Ovid the cynical realist”. Roy Gibson, in your own words: Book 3 steers women to a middle way.

Greek sculpture (in your own words; do not invent quotations). E. H. Gombrich: the Greek revolution. Greek artists, unlike Egyptian ones, kept correcting the inherited formula against what they saw, so the figure becomes more lifelike with each generation. J. J. Pollitt: fifth-century sculpture shows character (ethos), calm and general; the fourth century turns to emotion and mood (pathos). Andrew Stewart: the Aphrodite of Knidos creates the female nude, and with it the question of who is looking.

7 · Written answers

Five ten-mark answers, one for each topic. You write a 30-mark essay every Thursday, so the writing here is the other kind of question: short, exact, with no introduction and no conclusion. Do the first three in Week 1 and the last two in Week 2. Have the text or the picture in front of you for each. After the five ten-mark answers there are five responses to a scholar, one for each topic, from tab 6: four or five sentences each (claim, for, against, judgement).

[today’s date] A ten-mark answer
1. Plan five or six pairs. A pair = a point + a reference (a quotation, a line, a detail you can see).
2. Every pair has a because. Say what the reference shows. A pair with no because earns the reference mark only.
3. Cover the whole source: references from the start, the middle and the end, or from every part of the statue or scene.
4. No retelling, no introduction, no conclusion. Start with your first point.
5. No point should depend on one English word of a translation.
6. 200 to 250 words, about twelve minutes.
AO1 5 marks for the references, AO2 5 marks for what you say they show.

For each question: plan the pairs in the small box, write the answer, reveal the model, and mark yourself honestly: 2 if you have five pairs, each with a because, from across the source; 1 if you have three or four, or the becauses are thin; 0 if not done. Mr Beasley’s AI checker reads these afterwards. For a response to a scholar: 2 if you have the claim in your own words, evidence for and against, and a judgement; 1 if one of those is missing; 0 if not done.
